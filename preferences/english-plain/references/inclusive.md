# Inclusive, trauma-informed, and global writing

## People

- Put the person first: "people with disabilities", not "the disabled" or "handicapped". Follow a community's own preference where you know it. Some Deaf and autistic communities prefer identity-first language.
- Do not describe people with stigmatizing words: crazy, insane, dumb, lame, crippled, abnormal, "suffering from", "victim of". Ordinary technical terms such as "weak password" and "slow connection" are fine.
- Avoid ableist metaphors such as "blind spot", "falling on deaf ears", and "turn a blind eye".
- Do not assume a single norm, such as a "normal family" or a "normal user".
- Do not assume gender, marital status, age, or family structure unless the task needs it. Use singular "they" when gender is unknown.
- Do not use humor built on stereotypes about ethnicity, gender, religion, or bodies.

## Sensitive moments

- Do not blame. Not "You failed to enter a valid email." Write "Enter an email address like name@example.com."
- Say what will happen before it happens, and say whether it can be undone.
- Reassure only with facts the product or the source confirms. "Your draft is saved" helps only when it is true.
- Do not create urgency. State a deadline only when it is real, and state it plainly.
- For sensitive topics (mental health, money, legal matters, traumatic experiences), acknowledge the weight without dramatizing it. Offer a clear way out for a reader who does not want to continue.

## Numbers and dyscalculia

- When a sentence holds several figures, move them into a list or a table.
- Give context for a percentage when the source has both figures: "30% cheaper: from $100 to $70."
- Add a comparison for a large number only when the source provides one. Otherwise suggest to the user that one would help.
- Show the steps of any calculation the reader must do. If a calculator would help, recommend one to the user.
- In important instructions, use sequence words as well as numerals: "First", "Second", "Finally".
- Say "noon" and "midnight", not "12 p.m." and "12 a.m.", which readers confuse.

## Global readers

- Do not assume the reader lives in a big city or a high-income setting, or has fast internet, a new phone, or a bank account.
- For second-language readers and translation tools, use short sentences, literal wording, and one meaning for each word.
- Use examples and names that reflect the audience, not only Western or urban ones.
- Avoid references that depend on one culture: sports metaphors, holidays, seasons, and local brands.
- When a service reaches a specific region, recommend a translation or a note in the local language for urgent instructions.
